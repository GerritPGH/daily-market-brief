Section 1What matters today

The US Treasury Secretary put a word to it yesterday: the bond market has a fever (Scott Bessent, quoted by Bloomberg, 8 Sep). The diagnosis worth having is that the fever is not American. It is Japanese. The yen has held its break — 153.40 this morning, a seven-month high, after last week’s move survived a strong US payroll print that would once have crushed it — and the reason matters more than the level: Norway’s $2.3tn sovereign fund proposed on 4 September to cut the government-bond share of its portfolio, in a way read as shifting out of Treasuries and toward newly competitive Japanese yields, and Japan’s own quasi-national asset managers are now nursing enough JGB losses to face what would be their first forced selling since 2011 (Bloomberg Points of Return, 9 Sep). If the world’s cheapest funding currency stops funding the world, every long end reprices — and three of ours already are: the thirty-year gilt at 5.82%, the US thirty-year at 5.25%, the South African ten-year up six basis points to 8.805%. Layer on the oil: US forces struck targets near Kharg Island, Iran’s main export terminal, and Brent is $99.26. South Africa met that shock with a contraction — Q2 GDP −0.2% q/q against −0.1% expected, annual growth halved to 0.9% from 1.9% — while its stock market went up 0.40% on a copper-and-crude resources bid. Two dates settle the week: the ECB tomorrow, hiking as the only G7 central bank still tightening, and US CPI on Friday, into a Fed whose chair has turned hawkish.

Section 2World at a glance

Asia amber

The yen is now the region’s main event and it is no longer a positioning story alone. Overnight rate expectations imply the Bank of Japan takes policy up a full percentage point to 1.9% over twelve months, with a hike expected next week; nominal wage growth is the strongest in decades and Q2 GDP was +0.4%, a third consecutive quarter of expansion (Investec/Bloomberg, 8–9 Sep). Gavekal’s Anatole Kaletsky calls the yen “more undervalued than any major currency in modern history” (opinion). Equities are paying for it: the Nikkei fell 1.71% on Tuesday and is a further 0.28% lower this morning. China is the offsetting strength — August exports +25% y/y to $401.4bn and a $119.1bn surplus on semiconductors, high-tech goods and vehicles — and offshore renminbi borrowing hit a record high.

UK amber

Two structural stories, neither of them about today’s tape. First, the gilt curve is still the developed world’s most expensive: 5.190% at ten years, 5.82% at thirty, and the ten-year rose again this morning while the US fell. Second, and the one worth ten minutes: Anthropic declined to give Britain’s AI Security Institute pre-release access to its newest model, the first time the UK’s flagship testing agency has been excluded, with Whitehall reading it as US AI protectionism arriving (FT, 9 Sep). On the corporate side Revolut is going after FTSE 250 corporate banking clients from Barclays and HSBC, targeting 1mn business customers globally by 2027, and McLaren committed £450mn and 1,000 jobs to Woking.

South Africa red

The decoupling flagged here yesterday resolved — badly for the economy, well for the index. Q2 GDP contracted 0.2% q/q against consensus of −0.1%, and annual growth halved to 0.9% from 1.9%, with manufacturing and mining the drags; Business Day’s framing is that this puts the Reserve Bank in a bind, since oil at $99 argues against the cut the growth number argues for. The JSE ignored it: All Share +0.40%, with Energy +3.81% and Chemicals +3.69% on crude, Glencore +4.53% to a new high, Anglo +3.98%, BHP +3.01%, against AngloGold −2.50%. The tell is underneath: Sibanye-Stillwater filed a Section 189A retrenchment consultation the same day. Old Mutual’s H1 operating EPS rose 11% with an R1bn buyback; China opened its market to South African cherries.

All Share 117,461.57 (+0.40%) · Top 40 110,035.41 (+0.39%) · SA 10y 8.805% · USD/ZAR 16.0504 · Brent $99.26

USA amber

Back from the long weekend and straight down: the S&P lost 0.58% to 7,674, the Nasdaq 100 0.12%, with Brent nearing $100 the stated cause. The policy setup has genuinely inverted — Chair Kevin Warsh’s hawkish remarks have the market pricing a near-term hike, not a cut, and Bessent’s buyback intervention was explicitly aimed at “quelling market fever”. PPI Thursday, CPI Friday, FOMC next week. Two AI-financing items matter more than the tape: bankers are pushing for investment-grade ratings for OpenAI and Anthropic ahead of their IPOs to open the corporate bond market to them, and AI-related issuers now account for more than a quarter of Swiss franc corporate bond issuance this year. Washington also banned Canadian dairy, motorcycles and most alcohol imports, effective in three weeks.
