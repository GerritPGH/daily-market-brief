Section 1What matters today

The bond market has already voted, and this evening the Federal Reserve is expected to agree with it: the ten-year US Treasury note traded at 5.033% yesterday, its highest since 2007, and futures put the probability of a quarter-point rise tonight at about 93% — with Morgan Stanley joining Goldman Sachs in an eleventh-hour switch to forecasting a hike, and Deutsche Bank calling a hold “the biggest dovish surprise at a scheduled FOMC meeting on record”. The Bank of England follows tomorrow at 3.75% into a labour market near a five-year-high unemployment rate, and the Bank of Japan on Friday with what the WSJ calls the fastest hike of its cycle. Three decisions in seventy-two hours, and the thing forcing all three is a supply shock: Brent above $107, and US diesel at a record $6.27 a gallon — a refining-margin record, not a crude-price record. Not one basis point anywhere will produce a single additional barrel. The second-order damage is already showing up in places the headlines are not looking: junk-rated borrowers are pulling $13bn+ of refinancings forward to get ahead of the hikes, the most crowded equity positioning of the year is unwinding at thirteen dollars of tech-ETF inflow for every one dollar elsewhere, and South Africa’s Central Energy Fund has already, quietly, fixed a record October petrol price — in which the rand is helping and every cent of the R2.41 comes from a refining margin set on another continent.

Section 6Opportunity Radar

Situations worth research time, ranked by the size of the price move relative to the change in fundamentals, by durability, and by relevance to the four markets. Research ideas only — never recommendations.

A known October cost shock against a market pricing an SA consumer recovery

Horizon: 4–8 weeks · Confidence: medium · South Africa

The R2.41/l October petrol increase is already effectively determined by first-half-September data, and roughly a third of the South African consumer basket is transport-sensitive. Yet the JSE closed higher yesterday on financials and life insurance (+0.99% and +2.05%), and Pepkor — which actually sells to that consumer — guided to HEPS of −1.0% to +9.0% and fell 2.3%.

The gap worth researching is between a dated, arithmetic Q4 cost shock and a market that is pricing SA domestic normalisation.

Wrong if: the CEF end-September snapshot reverses sharply, or Treasury uses the slate account or the RAF levy to smooth the gazette before 7 October.

Watch: the CEF end-September under-recovery print, and the split between the product line and the rand line.

The most crowded positioning of the year is in the thing with the weakest near-term catalyst

Horizon: weeks · Confidence: medium · USA

$52bn into tech ETFs since the late-March low against $4bn into the rest of the market — thirteen to one (Baird Strategas via Bloomberg, 15/9). Nearly $13bn went into a single Nasdaq-100 tracker last month alone.

The fundamentals have not broken: chip earnings over the summer were strong. But the SOX is still more than 20% below its June high, which tells you the move is being driven by flow and by the expected path of AI capex, not by delivered results. A crowded position with an unresolved policy question above it is the classic setup for the pain trade.

Wrong if: the Fed hikes and the market reads it as confirmation of growth rather than of inflation, and megacap earnings hold the multiple up.

Watch: the first week of net outflow from the large Nasdaq-100 tracker, and whether SOX makes a lower low while the S&P does not.

High-yield issuers are telling you where rates go, and they are voting with balance sheets

Horizon: this quarter · Confidence: medium-high · Cross-market / credit

At least $13bn of junk refinancings are being pulled forward, many earlier than the borrower would normally go, explicitly to get ahead of hikes. Europe’s high-yield market is set for its busiest week since June.

For anyone auditing or diligencing private-credit and PE portfolio companies, this quietly rewrites the maturity wall and the covenant profile carried into year-end: cheaper certainty now, a higher coupon locked in for longer. It also means the ‘2027 wall’ charts everyone has been using are already stale.

Wrong if: the Fed hikes and simultaneously signals that it is done, in which case the window stays open and later issuers get better spreads than the ones who rushed.

Watch: high-yield issuance volume for the rest of September and whether new deals start pricing wide of talk.

Two of the largest allocators in the world now disagree in public about emerging markets

Horizon: weeks to months · Confidence: low-medium · Cross-market

On 15 September BlackRock moved emerging-market equities back to overweight, citing strong earnings and the AI boom. On the same day the WSJ ran the opposite trade: the dollar could extend gains on higher US rates and higher oil.

Both cannot be comfortable. Historically a rising dollar plus a rising oil price is the single most reliable headwind to EM equity, and the AI-earnings argument is precisely the one being questioned in the tech tape this week.

Wrong if: the Fed hikes and the dollar sells the fact, which would resolve the contradiction in EM’s favour immediately.

Watch: the dollar index and EM equity fund flows in the five sessions after tonight’s decision, not before.

Data centres are quietly changing what a real-estate bond is

Horizon: quarters · Confidence: low-medium · USA / real estate

Bloomberg reports the AI data-centre boom is reshaping the CMBS market (14/9). The collateral is single-tenant, very long-lease and credit-linked to a handful of hyperscalers — which makes it less a property exposure than a corporate-credit exposure wearing a property wrapper.

For real-estate fund valuation that is a live question about comparables, cap-rate inputs and tenant concentration, not a credit-desk curiosity. Yesterday’s Cape Town appeal against an Equinix approval adds the other half: planning and environmental risk is now a real underwriting input, including in Africa.

Wrong if: hyperscaler capex commitments lengthen further and the leases genuinely do behave like infrastructure, in which case the re-rating is correct rather than a mispricing.

Watch: new-issue data-centre CMBS spreads against traditional industrial, and any first sign of a lease renegotiation.
