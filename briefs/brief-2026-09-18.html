Section 1What matters today

Three central banks moved inside thirty-six hours and only one of them got the currency it wanted. The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate a quarter point to 1.25% — the highest since April 1995 — on a 7–2 vote, and the yen promptly fell to 157.03, because a hike that was fully expected buys nothing (Nikkei Asia, 18/9; FT panel 18/9 05:30). The Bank of England held at 3.75% on a 6–3 vote, warned it may have to raise if energy prices stay high, and in the same breath rewrote quantitative tightening: of a £488.2bn gilt portfolio it will now sell only the £146bn maturing 2035–2049, at £20bn a year to around 2034, holding £222bn of shorts to maturity and permanently retaining £120bn of the longest paper to back the note issue (Bank of England Market Notice, 17/9). The ten-year gilt fell 11bp to 5.24%; the thirty-year to 5.76%. And in New York the market spent a day deciding that Kevin Warsh’s first hike in three years was credibility rather than panic: the S&P 500 closed +1.14% at 7,637.76 and the Nasdaq 100 +1.73%, the biggest gains in six weeks, with the ten-year Treasury back under five per cent at 4.94%. Underneath all of it, two quieter items deserve your attention more than the rate decisions do: Turkey’s regulator froze trading in funds run by seven asset managers and ordered 130 funds liquidated (FT, 18/9), and bond managers began saying out loud that the next supply shock is food, not energy — which matters because the SARB meets on 23 September with the repo at 7.00% after a 4–2 hold.
